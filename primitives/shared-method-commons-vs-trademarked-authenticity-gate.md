# Shared-method commons / trademarked authenticity gate

Definition
--
Shared-method commons / trademarked authenticity gate is the mechanic where a movement decides who owns the right to define, carry, and validate the method. In the shared-method-commons form, the core practice feels like a stewarded inheritance: members, local rooms, and lightweight service bodies keep it legible, but legitimacy does not depend mainly on legal ownership of the doctrine. In the trademarked-authenticity-gate form, the movement ties legitimacy to protected marks, approved channels, and institutional guardianship over what counts as the real method. Same continuity problem, different ownership answer. Commons-oriented systems rely more on recognizable practice, social inheritance, and light stewardship. Trademark-gated systems rely more on legal control, orthodoxy enforcement, and institutional permission to decide what is authentic.

One-paragraph example
--
AA leans toward shared-method commons. Its local groups are autonomous, its membership threshold is thin, and A.A. World Services / GSO mainly steward literature and service infrastructure rather than owning sobriety itself as a branded product. Authority lives in the Steps, Traditions, the Big Book, sponsors, long-timers, and group conscience. Scientology leans toward a trademarked authenticity gate. Its founder scripture, exactness norms, training standards, and RTC-held marks preserve what is framed as authentic technology, so legitimacy is more tightly bound to official institutional control. Same durability problem, opposite ownership posture. AA says: the method is a fellowship inheritance carried in rooms, literature, and peers. Scientology says: the method is authentic insofar as it is preserved, guarded, and applied through the official chain.

Why it matters
--
- Determines whether legitimacy is earned mainly through recognizable practice and peer continuity or granted through legal/institutional ownership of authenticity.
- Changes how drift and correction work: commons can sprawl or mutate, while trademark gates can preserve exactness but also freeze doctrine and make dissent look counterfeit.
- Shapes scale economics and access because member-carried commons spread cheaply, while authenticity-gated systems concentrate more power in guardian institutions.
- Affects founder-afterlife durability: commons reduce single-owner dependence, while trademarked guardianship can keep founder authority alive long after death.
- Reveals whether the movement treats the method as a shared inheritance to steward or as protected spiritual/intellectual property to control.

Shadow risk
--
This primitive can fail in both directions. Shared-method commons can drift, fragment, or leave too much room for local confusion and uneven quality control. Trademarked authenticity gates fail harder: legal ownership can become a substitute for truth, institutional guardians can monopolize legitimacy, and deviation can be treated as corruption simply because it sits outside the official marks. The healthy question is whether stewardship keeps the method usable and trustworthy, or whether ownership has become the real source of authority.

Safe translation
--
If a product, community, or movement borrows this mechanic, prefer open stewardship over ownership capture. Publish the method clearly, define what must stay intact, and use versioning, attribution, and training norms before reaching for hard authenticity monopolies. Protect names if needed, but do not make legal control the main proof that the method works. The useful pattern is a shared protocol with clear stewardship. The dangerous pattern is turning legitimacy into intellectual-property enclosure.
