# Local-group sovereignty / central command hierarchy

Definition
--
Local-group sovereignty / central command hierarchy is the mechanic where a movement decides who actually holds decision rights once the system spreads beyond a single room. On one end, the movement gives substantial authority to local groups: rooms can govern themselves, adapt practice within broad guardrails, and resolve many questions through shared norms, elected servants, or group conscience. The center exists, but mainly as a service layer for literature, light coordination, and continuity. On the other end, the movement builds a command hierarchy: local units are real, but they sit inside a stronger chain of authority that can define orthodoxy, enforce procedure, route correction upward, and preserve alignment through managerial layers. Same scaling problem, different answer. Local sovereignty produces resilience, low-friction participation, and less total institutional capture. Central command produces tighter coherence, faster correction, and stronger continuity under pressure, but it also increases the risk that members begin relating to the organization as a ruling authority rather than as a shared method.

One-paragraph example
--
AA leans toward local-group sovereignty. Its local meetings are the real site of belonging and recovery, and the fellowship’s formal language emphasizes group conscience, autonomous groups, and trusted servants who “do not govern.” There is shared literature and service infrastructure, but the center is not meant to function like a command chain. Scientology leans toward central command hierarchy. Local missions and churches exist, but they operate inside a more explicit management structure with ethics personnel, supervisory layers, international management, trademark-controlled orthodoxy, and elite enforcement/continuity bodies like Sea Org and RTC. Same coordination challenge, opposite answer. AA says the room carries the program and the center serves it. Scientology says the room exists inside a larger command architecture that preserves alignment from above.

Why it matters
--
- Determines whether belonging feels primarily peer-held and local or institutionally supervised from higher levels.
- Changes the movement’s failure mode: local-sovereignty systems drift, fragment, or vary in quality; command-hierarchy systems overconcentrate power and can turn disagreement into noncompliance.
- Shapes member psychology because a command chain teaches people where final judgment lives.
- Affects scale durability: central hierarchy can preserve exact coherence across distance, while local sovereignty relies more on culture, literature, and norms to stay legible.
- Reveals the governance cost of order: tighter alignment usually requires more administrative power, more enforcement capacity, and clearer authority gradients.

Shadow risk
--
This primitive breaks in both directions. Local-group sovereignty can drift into inconsistency, hidden local abuse, vague accountability, or fragmentation when the center refuses to intervene even when intervention is actually needed. Central command hierarchy goes bad when coherence becomes domination: local leaders stop being stewards and become enforcers, dissent is treated as disobedience, and the organization gains too much power over how members interpret conflict, truth, and legitimacy. The key question is whether the governance shape exists to support the method or to subordinate members to the structure.

Safe translation
--
If a product, community, or movement borrows this mechanic, default to strong local autonomy with explicit minimum standards, transparent escalation paths, and narrow reasons for central intervention. Let the center handle shared tools, safety baselines, and doctrine clarity, but do not let it become the automatic owner of every dispute, interpretation, or leadership question. The useful pattern is service infrastructure plus legible local stewardship. The dangerous pattern is hidden fiefdoms on one side and total managerial capture on the other.
