# Primitive: Compact doctrine (example card)

Definition
--
Compact doctrine is the mechanic where a movement compresses a worldview into a small set of short, repeatable distinctions that members can remember, quote, and apply in real time. Instead of asking people to hold a full theory in working memory, the system gives them portable verbal handles. Those handles do double duty: they simplify teaching and they become self-diagnosis tools that change what members notice inside their own lives.

One-paragraph example
--
Landmark is a clean example of compact doctrine because it turns a broad transformation pitch into a handful of branded distinctions: `Already Always Listening`, `Context`, `What happened vs the story`, `Vicious Circle`, and `Rackets`. A participant does not need to carry the whole curriculum at once. They only need to recognize that they are "in a racket," that a hidden `context` is shaping action, or that they are confusing what happened with the story about what happened. That compression makes the doctrine portable. Members can reuse the language in the room, in dyads, and in outside conversations, which lets the movement spread not just ideas but a reusable interpretation layer.

Why it matters
--
- Lowers cognitive friction: a few portable phrases travel farther than a full explanatory system.
- Accelerates pattern recognition: members can label experience quickly instead of starting from scratch each time.
- Makes teaching scalable: leaders, volunteers, and graduates can reproduce the same core ideas with less drift.
- Creates in-group fluency: shared language becomes a belonging signal as well as a learning tool.
- Bridges private change and social spread: the same phrase that helps a member interpret life also helps them explain the system to others.
- Increases retrieval under pressure: short handles survive emotional intensity better than long theory.

Shadow risk
--
This mechanic becomes dangerous when compressed language stops clarifying reality and starts replacing it. Short handles can become thought-terminating labels, insider shibboleths, or tools for winning arguments without real examination. The tighter the vocabulary, the easier it is to overfit human complexity into the system's preferred categories. Once members can explain everything too quickly, the doctrine can become self-sealing.

Safe translation
--
Use compact doctrine as a clarity aid, not a totalizing map. Keep the phrases short, plain, and operational; pair every distinction with real-world examples and permission to reject it if it does not fit lived experience. A healthy translation sounds like: "Here are three short handles that may help you notice the pattern faster — keep them only if they improve your judgment." The goal is legibility and portability, not linguistic capture.

Small-risk-mitigation checklist
--
- Keep the vocabulary small enough to aid memory, not dominate thought.
- Require concrete examples before treating a distinction as true.
- Avoid jargon that mainly signals status or insider belonging.
- Make it easy for members to say, "that label doesn't fit this case."
- Revisit whether the language improves judgment or merely speeds agreement.
- Prefer interpretable language over trademark theater when possible.
