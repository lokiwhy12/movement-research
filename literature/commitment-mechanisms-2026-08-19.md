## 1. Claims, in the authors' terms

Kanter (opened). Commitment is an organizational problem, not a personality trait. A group has to solve three problems at once: keeping people in (instrumental / continuance), holding them together (affective / cohesion), and making group demands feel right (moral / control). Each problem is a pair: one process detaches the person from a prior stake, one attaches them to the group (Ch.3, pp.86–89). The independent variable is how existence is ordered — “specific ways of ordering and defining the existence of a group,” ideals turned into concrete practices (Ch.4, p.90). The dependent variables named at the close of Ch.3 are endurance and member satisfaction (p.89). Duration in the sample is operationalized as at least 25 years (Appendix, p.260).

Instrumental claim, in her terms: profits of membership have to exceed costs; sacrifice raises the psychic expense of having joined, so members justify staying by belief (p.91). Affective claim: emotional commitment becomes commitment to a set of social relationships (p.87); renunciation drops competing ties and communion produces we-feeling (p.88). Moral claim: evaluative orientations are redefined so group demands seem right; mortification detaches, transcendence attaches; mortification opens the person to new directions and transcendence defines those directions (p.89).

Iannaccone (body not opened). From the opened 1992 abstract and from later restatements opened in the prior run: religion is modeled as a club good with positive returns to participatory crowding; agents are not assigned special religious motives; stigma, self-sacrifice, and behavioral restrictions can be efficient for fully rational members because they tax outside activity and reduce free-riding. The model is stated as extending to nonreligious clubs. The independent variable is the price attached to outside activity. The dependent variable is equilibrium participation and club-good quality, not felt loyalty. That is not “suffering makes you loyal.”

Do not collapse the two. Kanter’s units are six organizational arrangements indexed to three problems. Iannaccone’s units are a price on substitutes plus two channels (substitution and screening) as later writers name them. Costly signaling proper, as Sosis restates Irons, is a third claim (hard-to-fake information about credibility). It is not Kanter and it is not, in the same terms, Iannaccone.

## 2. Named mechanisms

Kanter’s six, from the opened pairing (Ch.3 pp.86–89; map at Ch.4 p.91). The sixth name in the book is transcendence, not surrender.

1. Sacrifice (instrumental, detach). Abstinence and austerity raise the cost of having joined so continuance is justified by belief.
2. Investment (instrumental, attach). Financial and property arrangements put a stake in the group; successful groups in the sample generally did not reimburse seceders (p.96).
3. Renunciation (affective, detach). Competing ties are dropped: insulating boundaries, weakened couple/family claims, in/out language, institutional completeness (including medical services, p.99). Free love and celibacy both appear as group regulation of intimacy (p.102).
4. Communion (affective, attach). Homogeneous backgrounds, communal property and pooled income, shared work, frequent group contact, ritual. Produces we-feeling (pp.88, 110–116).
5. Mortification (moral, detach). Private states are submitted; former identity is exchanged. Mutual criticism, surveillance, probationary socialization, confession (pp.122–125).
6. Transcendence (moral, attach). Decision-making is attached to a greater power. Institutionalized awe, insulated hierarchy, non-participative decisions, tradition as the authority of the eternal yesterday (pp.128–138).

Iannaccone’s named terms, from opened abstract and restatement only (not from the 1992 body): club good; participatory crowding; sacrifice and stigma as paired instruments. Substitution and screening are later labels for the two channels, not verified as his section headings.

## 3. Evidence type and sample

Kanter (opened Appendix). Population starts from Bestor’s list: 124 names, then 91 utopian communities founded in the United States 1780–1860. Defined by mutually expressive goals and identity of organization, not by communist or separatist as such (pp.256–259). 11 successful, 79 unsuccessful, 1 unclassifiable (Icaria) (p.260). Success = at least 25 years. Oneida is counted as 33 years. No unsuccessful case exceeded 16 years (p.260). Coded sample: 9 successful and 21 unsuccessful with at least two independent sources. Fourierist groups undersampled. Five judges, 260 questions, interjudge r = .79 (pp.260–265). Tables 1–6 are presence counts, not weighted strength. Opened table lines: sacrifice / celibacy 9/9 successful vs 2/21 unsuccessful (Table 1, p.95); renunciation / free love or celibacy 100% vs 29% (Table 3, p.107); communion / common religious background 89% vs 50% (Table 4, p.119).

Iannaccone 1992: formal model. Body not opened. The 1994 strictness paper (empirics) was not opened.

Opened in the prior Deep Research run, still secondary-or-extension relative to this session’s required pair:
- Sosis and Bressler (2003), full PDF: 83 communes with enough information (30 religious, 53 secular) from Oved’s list; 22 costly requirements; 1,556 commune-years. Extension, not Kanter, not Iannaccone.
- Carvalho (2015) working paper, full PDF: restatement of the club model plus sorting.

## 6. Limits and overclaims

From Kanter’s own opened pages, not only from later critics:
- The coded tables are presence/absence. They do not weight mechanism strength or track change over a group’s life (implied by how Tables 1–6 are built).
- Success is a 25-year cut, not a continuous duration correlation (Appendix, p.260). Oneida is 33 years; no unsuccessful case in the coded set lasted past 16.
- Fourierist groups are undersampled (p.260). Generalizing “all utopian experiments” past that construction overclaims the appendix.
- Ch.9 names live limits inside her own frame: shirkers as a real problem (p.235); austerity and ecstasy as cycles (p.237); anti-intellectualism not required (p.239); a preference for commitment to process rather than structure (p.239); contribution to culture rather than politics (p.241); scale past a few hundred requires change (p.243); federation versus bureaucracy (p.245); dependence on the outside society (p.245). Those are her limits, not a later smear.

From opened secondaries (still labeled secondary):
- Rubin et al. (2019): present-day intentional communities often lack her mechanisms; mechanism counts did not predict their satisfaction measure. Her list may not be portable off the 1780–1860 coded sample.
- Hall (1988), unread as primary: secondaries report two pathways (ethnic bounded-status group; otherworldly shared belief) rather than one commitment effect. That remains a second-hand limit until Hall is opened.
- Sosis and Bressler (2003): costly requirements predicted longevity for religious communes, not secular ones. Iannaccone’s suggestion that strictness should travel to secular clubs is not licensed by that dataset. Costliness did not predict dissolution type. Cost is not monotonic; the most-requirement communes were not the longest-lived.

Neither tradition licenses a design instruction. Kanter is comparative-historical on a defined 19th-century sample. Iannaccone (as known from abstract and restatement) is an equilibrium club-good model. Neither was estimated on a contemporary consumer, church, or brand population in the pages opened here.

The two traditions remain different claims after the book was opened. Confirming the six names and the three-problem pairing does not turn Kanter into costly signaling.

## 7. Open questions a later packet-supplement could test against a real case

Questions only. No scores. No new primitive names. No case named here.

1. Does the case show arrangements that map onto continuance, cohesion, and control as three separate problems, or only one of them?
2. Which of the six named mechanisms are even codeable there, and which are structurally unavailable (as Rubin et al. found in present-day communes)?
3. If sacrifice is present, is it Kanter’s psychic-expense / belief-justification path, Iannaccone’s price on outside activity, or only something expensive?
4. Do substitution and screening separate, or is neither observable?
5. Is there a club good with participatory crowding, or a private good that is merely co-consumed?
6. Does mortification in the case look like confession/criticism/probation (her map), or is that word being used loosely?
7. Is transcendence institutionalized awe and insulated hierarchy (pp.128–134), or only a slogan about meaning?
8. What is the case’s analogue of “25 years,” and would a different cut change the reading (her own unsuccessful ceiling was 16)?
9. Could prior-network or ethnic homogeneity (Hall’s unread pathway, secondary only) account for cohesion without the six-mechanism story?
10. What would falsify a commitment-mechanism reading of the case, stated in advance?
