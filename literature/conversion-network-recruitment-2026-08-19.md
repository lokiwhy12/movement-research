TL;DR. Lofland and Stark (1965) proposed a seven-condition, sequential “value-added” funnel for conversion — three predisposing background conditions (tension; religious problem-solving perspective; religious seekership) plus four situational contingencies (turning point; cult affective bond; weak/neutralized extra-cult attachments; intensive interaction). It rests on one small movement (full data on 15 of 21 converts, early-1960s West Coast). The authors flagged limited generalizability themselves.

Stark’s network-recruitment argument (Stark and Bainbridge 1980 abstract; restated in Stark 1996 JCR, opened) makes the independent variable the relative balance of interpersonal attachments: people convert when ties to members outweigh ties to nonmembers, with belief following attachment rather than preceding it. Deprivation/ideology and networks are cast as complementary, not competing.

The popular paraphrase “people join because their friends join” is directionally faithful but overstates simplicity (it drops the affective-bond-can-form-with-strangers clause and the overbalance ratio) and understates limits (networks do not much govern diffuse occult belief; the models describe entry, not what happens after).

## 1. Claims, in the authors' terms

Lofland and Stark 1965 (primary, full text opened). Aim: a model of the conversion process through which a group of people came to see the world in terms set by the doctrines of one obscure and devalued perspective — a small millenarian religious cult. The model is a value-added conception borrowed from Neil Smelser: seven successively accumulating factors, which in their total combination seem to account for conversion. All seven are said to be individually necessary and jointly sufficient. The imagery is a funnel that systematically reduces the number of persons available for recruitment.

Two degrees of conversion: verbal converts (fellow-travelers who profess belief but take no active role) and total converts (who demonstrate commitment through deeds as well as words, becoming a deployable agent). The first six conditions produce verbal conversion; the seventh (intensive interaction) is what converts a verbal convert into a total convert.

Ordering is by activation, not strict temporal occurrence: a condition may have existed long before or may arise just in time, and the temporal order may vary.

The most-quoted line, in their terms: “In a manner of speaking, final conversion was coming to accept the opinions of one's friends” (p. 871). They downgrade prior ideological congruence: only the two “gross” points of congruence bundled into religious seekership were necessary; specifics such as fundamentalism, millenarianism, or hallucinatory experience were far from universal among pre-converts, and most became apocalyptic pre-millenarian only upon conversion.

The article studies “Divine Precepts” (D.P.) under pseudonyms. Later annotation identifies D.P. as the Unification Church's Divine Principle. The 1965 article itself keeps names pseudonymous.

Stark network argument — keep separate. 1980 AJS abstract (primary as abstract; body not opened): a long tradition explains recruitment by congruence between ideology and deprivations; a more recent approach argues interpersonal bonds between members and potential recruits are the essential element; these are complementary, not competing; quantitative data on three religious groups show overwhelming support for the crucial role of social networks.

The independent variable is stated most crisply in later restatements: conversion occurs when people have or develop stronger attachments to members than to nonmembers — when attachments to members overbalance attachments to nonmembers. “Overbalance” as a word is from secondary quotation, not from an opened 1980 body.

Stark 1996 JCR (full text opened): people do not join because they suddenly found the doctrines appealing; they convert when their ties to members outweigh their ties to nonmembers. He pairs this with a boundary condition: movements must sustain strong internal ties while remaining an open social network, able to maintain and form ties to outsiders. A social implosion (all ties turned inward) halts growth.

Do not collapse 1965 into 1996. The 1965 claim is a seven-condition funnel on one group. The 1996 claim is a network-balance proposition (ties outweigh nonmembers) plus the open-network / social-implosion boundary. The 1980 body that sits between them was not opened.

## 2. Named mechanisms / conditions

Lofland–Stark 1965 (primary, opened). Three predisposing, then four situational.

Predisposing:
1. Tension — an acutely felt, enduring discrepancy between an imagined ideal and actual circumstances that creates a disposition to act (but does not determine which act).
2. Religious problem-solving perspective — problems are interpreted in religious rather than psychiatric or political terms, so a religious solution becomes thinkable.
3. Religious seekership — conventional religion found inadequate; the person defines themselves as a seeker among religious alternatives.

Situational:
4. Turning point — the encounter coincides with a moment (migration, job loss, school completion/failure, illness, marital change) when old lines of action are disrupted.
5. Cult affective bond — a positive emotional tie forms with (or pre-exists toward) one or more members.
6. Weak or neutralized extra-cult attachments — outside ties are absent, distant, or neutralized so they cannot pull the convert back.
7. Intensive interaction — daily, sustained, proximate contact with total converts transforms verbal assent into total commitment (deployable agent).

Stark / Stark and Bainbridge (1980 abstract + 1996 JCR full text). Four network items:
1. Interpersonal attachment balance — conversion follows the ratio of member-ties to nonmember-ties; when member-ties overbalance, conversion follows.
2. Networks as conduits — growth along pre-existing kin and friendship networks rather than broadcast or ideological appeal.
3. Open-network condition — internal cohesion plus retained outward ties; social implosion stops recruitment.
4. Belief-follows-attachment — doctrinal appeal is secondary; commitment to doctrine typically deepens after affiliation, not before.

## 3. Evidence type and sample

Lofland–Stark 1965 (primary). Qualitative participant observation plus interviews, early 1962 to mid-1963 (less intensive through mid-1964), one millenarian group (D.P.). 21 converts by mid-1963; full data on all seven factors for 15. Primarily white, Protestant, young (typically below 35), lower-middle-class small-town origins. Authors: because the model was developed from a small set of converts to a minor millenarian doctrine, it may possess few generalizable features.

Stark and Bainbridge 1980 (abstract only; body not opened). Abstract: earlier network evidence was limited and qualitative; they add quantitative data on three quite different radical religious groups — Unification Church, Mormons (LDS), and a kinship-based doomsday sect. In-text specifics (kin defection rates; Mormon cold-call vs in-home contact figures) were reached via secondary quotation and later Stark books, not from an opened 1980 body. Do not upgrade those numbers to a 1980-body finding.

Stark 1996 JCR (primary, full text opened). Restates the network proposition as proposition 8 in a ten-proposition model and cross-references the 1965 and 1980 studies.

The Rise of Christianity (1996), not opened cover-to-cover. One passage, reached as restatement: when missionaries make cold calls, conversion follows about once in a thousand calls; first contact in the home of a Mormon friend or relative is said to result in conversion 50 percent of the time. Stark there attributes the figures to The Future of Religion (1985), which was not opened. Those numbers are not confirmed from the 1980 AJS body.

## 6. Limits and overclaims

From the opened 1965 article, not only from later critics:
- It does not license a checklist reading. Sequential value-added funnel; order is by activation; seven items claimed necessary and jointly sufficient for this group. Authors warn it may possess few generalizable features.
- It does not license “brainwashing.” The mechanism is affective bonding, not coercion.
- It does not claim ideology is irrelevant — only that specific doctrinal congruence beyond generic seekership is unnecessary, and that acceptance of doctrine tends to follow the affective bond.
- Empirical base is one small group (N=15 with full data of 21 converts). No control group. Cannot support quantitative generalization alone.

Snow and Phillips 1980 (critique, partial; not Lofland–Stark): little empirical support for the model in its entirety on Nichiren Shoshu; affective and intensive interaction held; predisposing conditions and turning point fared poorly. Conversion in general is highly improbable in the absence of those two relational factors, on their account. This stays a partial/critique open. Do not upgrade it to a full-text finding.

Kox, Meeus and 't Hart 1991 (critique; full text not opened): fairly adequate as a set of conditions, inadequate as a process model (sequence does not reliably hold). Secondary/abstract only.

Lofland himself (1978, secondary only) reportedly said the model was an example of how to build a conversion model for a particular movement, not for wide application. Unread as primary.

Stark network argument does not claim networks explain everything. 1980 abstract: networks do not play an important role in mildly deviant occult beliefs (séances, tarot) that spread via mass media. That is the media-occult boundary.

Entry ≠ maintenance ≠ outcome. Both bodies explain recruitment/entry, not retention or internal life after.

Popular paraphrase “people join because their friends join” over-simplifies: omits that the affective bond can form with a stranger member, not only a prior friend; drops the overbalance ratio (relative attachment, not the mere presence of a friend); omits weakening of competing outside ties; understates the media-occult boundary and the entry-only scope.

Neither body licenses a design instruction. 1965 is a conversion model on one mid-1960s group. 1996 JCR is a general model of movement success/failure in which network balance is one proposition. Neither was used here to score a contemporary case.

## 7. Open questions a later packet-supplement could test against a real case

Questions only. No scores. No new primitive names. No case named here.

1. Does entry run through pre-existing kin/friendship ties, bonds formed with previously unknown members, or both — and in what proportion?
2. Can overbalance be operationalized: member-ties measured relative to nonmember-ties at joining?
3. Does doctrinal assent precede or follow the affective bond?
4. Which of the seven Lofland–Stark conditions are present, and does activation order match the funnel or only co-presence?
5. Do the two conditions that survived critique (affective bond, intensive interaction) appear necessary while the predisposing three vary?
6. Is there evidence of the open-network boundary — outward ties retained during growth, or inward closure?
7. Diffuse belief via media (where networks are claimed not to govern), organized-group recruitment, or a mix?
8. Is entry being conflated with retention or outcome in the available evidence?
