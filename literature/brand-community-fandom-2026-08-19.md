## 1. Claims, in the authors' terms

Muniz and O'Guinn (2001), "Brand Community" (full text opened). They introduce brand community as "a specialized, non-geographically bound community, based on a structured set of social relationships among admirers of a brand." It is specialized because a branded good or service sits at the center. Their central claim is that community — a core sociological construct long assumed destroyed by modernity, mass markets, and consumer culture — not only survives but can form around commercial objects; brand community is "neither any more nor less real" than other contemporary forms of community. Independent variable: a brand with (they argue) a strong image, a rich or lengthy history, and threatening competition, publicly consumed. Dependent claim: those conditions give rise to a genuine, if particular, community exhibiting three traditional markers — shared consciousness, rituals and traditions, and a sense of moral responsibility. They reposition the object of study from the consumer–brand dyad to a consumer–brand–consumer triad, endorsing Cova's line that "the link is more important than the thing." Brand communities are "explicitly commercial," self-aware about that commercialism, and they "embrace" rather than reject mainstream culture, unlike Hebdige-style oppositional subcultures. Limit they draw themselves: brand communities are "communities of limited liability" (Janowitz) — intentional, voluntary, partial — with a bounded, "limited and specialized" moral responsibility, and "relatively strong (but rarely extreme)" commitment.

Kozinets (2001), "Utopian Enterprise" (full text opened). Sample: the Star Trek culture of consumption. Central claim: a mass-media commercial text is negotiated by consumers into a "powerful utopian refuge" — a conceptual space for constructing a sense of self and of what matters. Independent variables: stigma, social situation, and the need for legitimacy. Dependent claim: these shape how diverse Star Trek subcultures build consumption meanings and practices, and specifically that fans deploy "sacralizing articulations" — describing Star Trek as a religion or myth — as a legitimacy strategy to distance heavy self-investment from the text's "superficial status as a commercial product." In the author's terms, religion/myth talk is data (a fan-side rhetorical move to manage stigma), not the researcher's claim that fandom is a religion. Key interpretive construct: Hall's articulation (linkages that are contingent, not necessary). Finding: pervasive internal heterogeneity ("elective affinities," after Weber) rather than one shared identity. He also documents the tension between a socially utopian / anti-capitalist text and its overt commercialism.

Later popular paraphrase (labeled secondary / practitioner — kept apart). Trade and popular writing recasts this literature as "brand cult," "customers become true believers," or "fandom is just religion" (e.g. Douglas Atkin, The Culting of Brands, listing Apple and Harley alongside the Hell's Angels and the Unification Church). This is not what the opened authors claim. Muniz and O'Guinn describe "relatively strong (but rarely extreme)" commitment and distance their members from "indiscriminate zealots." Kozinets treats "Star Trek as religion" as a fan legitimacy device, not a finding of conversion. The popularizations collapse bounded claims into a religion/NRM frame the source texts do not license.

## 2. Named mechanisms and structures

From Muniz and O'Guinn (2001):
- Consciousness of kind (after Gusfield): the intrinsic connection members feel toward one another and their collective difference from non-members — "a shared knowing of belonging."
- Legitimacy: members distinguish "true" members from those using the brand "for the wrong reasons."
- Oppositional brand loyalty: community and brand meaning derived through opposition to competing brands (their examples: Mac vs PC/Microsoft; Saab vs Volvo).
- Rituals and traditions: greeting rituals, celebrating brand history, sharing brand stories (named story-types: the odyssey, the saved-my-life, the magic cavern).
- Moral responsibility: felt duty to the collective — integrate and retain members; assist in proper use of the brand.
- Framing notes in their terms: imagined communities (Anderson); communities of limited liability (Janowitz); commercial and mass-mediated ethos.

From Kozinets (2001):
- Articulation / disarticulation / rearticulation (after Hall): contingent linkages among text, consumption practices, and meanings.
- Utopian refuge / sanctuary: the text constructed as a space of acceptance for the stigmatized.
- Stigma management: self-stigma, concealment/covering, and "appropriate detachment" as boundary-work.
- Sacralizing / legitimizing articulations: religion/myth talk used to elevate and defend investment.
- Elective affinities (after Weber): sub-groups selecting different facets of the text, producing heterogeneity rather than one identity.

Related named structures from works not opened in full (flagged, restatement-level): customer-centric four-relationship model (McAlexander, Schouten and Koenig 2002, abstract only); twelve common practices in four thematic aggregates — social networking, impression management, community engagement, brand use (Schau, Muñiz and Arnould 2009, abstract/snippet only).

## 3. Evidence type and sample

Muniz and O'Guinn (2001): interpretive/ethnographic plus computer-mediated communication. Two phases: face-to-face fieldwork in "Fairlawn," a 14-home neighborhood in Bloomingdale (pop. 115,000); four households interviewed two-to-four times monthly over 12 months; plus analysis of user- and club-created web pages (over 50 sites / over 300 pages). About 192 hours of interview and observational data. Named brand samples: Ford Bronco, Macintosh, and Saab. Sample over-represented relatively affluent white families. Ethnography does not afford probability-sample generalizability. Brands are the study's sample only; not scored.

Kozinets (2001): ethnography of the Star Trek culture of consumption — 20 months of fieldwork (1995–1997) across a local fan club ("FanTrek," Canada), five conventions, and online newsgroups/web/e-mail. 67 interviews with self-identified fans (two long in-person plus 65 e-mail); about 52 percent identifiable as female; e-mails from domains in the United States (69 percent), Canada, Brazil, Australia and elsewhere. Roughly 440,000 words of field notes/interviews, 260,000 words of artifactual data, 267 photographs. Star Trek is the media-text sample.

Arvidsson and Caliandro (2016): netnography plus digital methods (social-network / co-hashtag analysis in Gephi, content coding) on Italian-language Twitter about Louis Vuitton, 30 Sept–30 Nov 2013; 8,949 unique tweets (keyword 7,814 + hashtag 2,848, overlap 1,713; 10,662 gathered before de-duplication in the manuscript). Louis Vuitton is the sample only.

## 4. Primary vs secondary

Opened as the work itself (three): Muniz and O'Guinn 2001; Kozinets 2001; Arvidsson and Caliandro 2016, "Brand Public," JCR 42(5): 727–748. The two required primaries are satisfied. The demanded critique is satisfied by Arvidsson and Caliandro: it does not collapse brand community into religion or NRM interiors. Its axis is publicity/visibility versus identity/belonging. In their terms, much brand-related sociality on social media is better described as a "brand public": an organized media space held together by a continuity of mediation (a hashtag/platform) and by individual or collective affect, in which participants pursue publicity value (the brand as a medium giving visibility to personal identities that originate elsewhere) rather than the linking value / shared collective identity central to Muniz and O'Guinn. Evidence they name: retweet rate 4.5 percent; a fragmented network with near-total turnover of top users; tweet content split across sales, news, and self-presentation — no consciousness of kind, no rituals and traditions, little or no moral protagonism. They cite Muniz and O'Guinn 2001 and Muniz and Schau 2005 as the identity-based model they are moving away from. They make no religion/cult/conversion argument of their own.

Muniz and Schau 2005, "Religiosity in the Abandoned Apple Newton Brand Community": abstract only. Strongest peer-reviewed piece that does foreground religious/supernatural motifs; explicitly not used as the demanded non-religion critique.

Restatements / practitioner (not used to set authors'-terms claims): literature reviews, encyclopedia entries, teaching notes, and popular "brand cult" writing including Atkin.

## 6. Limits and overclaims

The opened texts do not license treating brand community as a religion, church, cult, or NRM interior. Muniz and O'Guinn describe bounded, limited-liability, rarely-extreme commitment and self-aware commercialism. Kozinets treats religion/myth talk as a fan legitimacy device — a contingent articulation — not evidence that fandom is a religion.

The opened texts do not license treating a commercial-object public as a convert class. Consciousness of kind, legitimacy, and oppositional loyalty are boundary and status processes, not conversion mechanisms. Arvidsson and Caliandro show a major brand formation (a brand public) with no shared identity at all.

The opened texts do not license importing commitment-mechanism lists, conversion funnels, or costly-signaling frames as the interpretive home of this literature.

Author-stated generalizability limits: Muniz and O'Guinn over-represent affluent white families; two of three brands had small market share. Kozinets notes a likely sampling skew (e-mail informants skewed toward the alienated and escape-seeking) and that religion/myth findings are fan articulations, not the researcher's ontology. Arvidsson and Caliandro: one brand, one national Twitter, about two months.

The strongest quantified apparatus in this lane (four-relationship model; twelve practices) comes from works not opened in full and stays restatement-level.

## 7. Open questions a later packet-supplement could test

Questions only. No scores. No new primitive names. Do not name the 23 as cases.

Under what conditions does consciousness of kind require an opponent (oppositional brand loyalty) versus persisting without one, as Muniz and O'Guinn found for Macintosh?

When does a commercial-object public behave as an interacting brand community versus a non-interacting brand public (Arvidsson and Caliandro), and what mediating conditions move a formation between the two?

How stable is moral responsibility as commitment scales — does it remain limited liability, or does it change form, in higher-share or mainstream brands?

Does the fan use of religion/myth talk documented by Kozinets function purely as stigma-management legitimacy, or does it ever index something more, and how would one tell the difference empirically?

How does internal heterogeneity (elective affinities) relate to community continuity — a question the un-opened Thomas, Price and Schau 2013 appears to engage?

Where do self-marking and recruiting sit on the interaction-versus-mediation axis when the formation is largely online?
