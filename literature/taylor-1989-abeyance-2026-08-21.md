## 1. Claims, in the author's terms

Taylor's named term is **abeyance**. In her words, the term "abeyance" depicts a holding process by which movements sustain themselves in nonreceptive political environments and provide continuity from one stage of mobilization to another (p. 761). She restates: "Abeyance is essentially a holding pattern of a group which continues to mount some type of challenge even in a nonreceptive political environment" (p. 772). She borrows the word from Mizruchi (1983), her citation: abeyance structures emerge "when society lacks sufficient status vacancies to integrate surpluses of marginal and dissident people"; they "temporarily retain potential challengers to the status quo" (p. 762). She challenges and extends him: social-movement abeyance organizations, "by providing a measure of continuity for challenging groups, also contribute to social change" (p. 762).

What she argues against is an **"immaculate conception"** view of 1960s origins, in which new social movements "seemingly emerged out of nowhere" (p. 761). Overemphasis on origins has blinded the field to the **"carry-overs and carry-ons"** between movements (Gusfield 1981, p. 324, as she cites; p. 761). What look like "births" were "breakthroughs or turning points in movement mobilization" (p. 761). Against the account that American feminism died after suffrage (1920) and was reborn in the 1960s, often from the civil rights movement, she treats the 1960s wave as a **resurgent challenge** with roots in an earlier cycle (p. 761).

The process characterizes "mass movements that succeed in building a support base and achieving a measure of influence, but are confronted with a nonreceptive political and social environment" (p. 762). As support falls, the most committed become "increasingly marginal and socially isolated"; if routine statuses cannot absorb them, "alternative structures emerge" and "a movement in abeyance becomes a cadre of activists who create or find a niche for themselves" (p. 762). Such groups "may have little impact in their own time" and may even help maintain the status quo, but they remain "sources of protest and change" (p. 762).

**External vs internal.** External: a pool of marginal potential activists, produced by changing opportunity structures plus "an absence of status vacancies to absorb dissident and excluded groups" (p. 762); restated as "a discrepancy between a surplus of activists and a lack of status opportunities for integrating them into the mainstream" (p. 772). Internal: five characteristics of movement abeyance structures, inductively derived and treated as continua — temporality, purposive commitment, exclusiveness, centralization, and culture (pp. 761, 765). She does not use "independent variable" or "dependent variable" (not recovered). Restated from her argument, not as her labels: the external discrepancy produces abeyance; the five internals permit holding; the dependent claim is continuity from one stage of mobilization to another, via organizational and ideological bridges between upsurges by the same challenging group (pp. 761, 772).

Instead of dying, in her words: "Most movements have thresholds or turning points in mobilization which scholars have taken for 'births' and 'deaths.'" Her claim: "movements do not die, but scale down and retrench to adapt to changes in the political climate. Perhaps movements are never really born anew. Rather, they contract and hibernate, sustaining the totally dedicated and devising strategies appropriate to the external environment" (p. 772). The task then shifts "from refining theories of movement emergence to accounting for fluctuations in the nature and scope of omnipresent challenges" (pp. 772–773).

Later popular paraphrase (labeled, kept apart). "Movements hibernate" as a slogan is not her sentence. Her own hibernate/contract wording is the scale-down-and-retrench claim above (p. 772); the slogan drops the retrenchment, the dedicated cadre, and the nonreceptive-climate condition.

Labeled contrast, once: abeyance is a holding pattern in a nonreceptive climate, not a system-wide phase of heightened conflict (Tarrow 2022 Ch. 8 names the latter as a cycle of contention). This note does not import Tarrow 2022 Ch. 8 mechanisms. She cites Tarrow 1983 for reform proceeding "in a ratchetlike fashion, where the gains of one struggle become the resources for later challenges by the same aggrieved group" (p. 772) — her citation, not an open of Tarrow 2011 Part III.

## 2. Named mechanisms and structures

Five internals (p. 761; elaborated pp. 765–770), treated as hypothetical continua derived from a single case (p. 765). One-sentence function each, in her terms.

1. **Temporality** — not mere persistence, but "the length of time that a movement organization is able to hold personnel" (p. 765). Function: "Constant numbers—even if small—are better for morale than steady turnover, so temporality enhances the likelihood that an organization will continue to endure" (p. 766).

2. **Purposive commitment** — "the willingness of people to do what must be done, regardless of personal rewards and sacrifices, to maintain a collective challenge" (p. 766). She cites Kanter (1972) that high-commitment groups are more likely to endure (her citation; Kanter not reopened). Function: commitment "functions as an obstacle to participation in alternative roles and organizations" (p. 767).

3. **Exclusiveness** — peak-mobilization organizations are inclusive; "in cycles of decline… organizations become exclusive and attempt to expel or hold constant their membership" (p. 767). She cites Zald and Ash (1966): exclusive SMOs are more likely to endure (her citation). NWP leadership preferred "a small elite vanguard"; one member: "We are an 'elect body' with a single point of agreement" (p. 767). Function: exclusiveness "ensures a relatively homogeneous cadre of activists suited to the limited activism undertaken" (p. 768).

4. **Centralization** — "a single center of power" versus decentralized subunits (Gamson 1975, p. 93, as she cites; p. 768). It can reduce direct-action tactics but produces "the organizational stability, coordination, and technical expertise necessary for movement survival" (p. 768). NWP: Alice Paul's direction; state branches often letterheads for lobbying (p. 768). Function: centralization "ensur[es] the maintenance of organization and at least minimal activity during periods when conditions do not favor mass mobilization" (p. 769).

5. **Culture** — "embodied in its collective emotions, beliefs, and actions" (p. 769). As the climate turns hostile, "organizations must elaborate alternative cultural frameworks to provide security and meaning" (p. 769). NWP culture at Alva Belmont House ("Woman's Party family"); feminism "defined principally through a culture that promoted a feminist worldview," not a fully articulated theory (pp. 769–770). Function: rich symbolic life "enhance[s] the abeyance function by helping to hold members" (p. 770).

She calls that mix — high longevity of attachment, intense purposive commitment, high exclusiveness, high centralization with specialized skills, rich political culture — "the ideal combination of factors necessary to hold a movement in abeyance until the external forces make it possible to resume a more mass-based challenge" (p. 770).

Three bridges from "organizational and ideological bridges between different upsurges of activism by the same challenging group" (p. 761). The NWP "provided preexisting activist networks, an existing repertoire of goals and tactics, and a collective identity that justified feminist opposition" (p. 770).

1. **Preexisting activist networks.** Holding-period personnel become usable later: NWP role in the President's Commission on the Status of Women (1961) and Title VII sex clause (1964); 4 of 10 original NOW Statement of Purpose signers; Friedan's "underground network" of longtime feminists (p. 770).

2. **Existing repertoire of goals and tactics.** The next wave inherits goals and forms — ERA, lobbying, a legal-equality frame — that both facilitate and constrain later action (pp. 770–771). Cost she names: the liberal 1960s branch "found it difficult to shake the class and race limitations of its predecessors" (p. 771).

3. **Collective identity.** A "sense of mission and moral purpose" (p. 762). In abeyance a SMO uses internally oriented activities "to build a structure through which it can maintain its identity, ideals, and political vision"; that constructed identity "can become an important symbolic resource for subsequent mobilizations" (p. 772).

## 3. Evidence type and sample

Single historical case: National Woman's Party, 1945 to mid-1960s, after suffrage left "no unifying goal" and the militant NWP campaigned for the ERA (extract; period also at pp. 761, 764). Data: NWP archives plus interviews; fuller documentation in Rupp and Taylor 1987 (her citation, p. 763). Sample only. Not scored. The 23 roster cases are not scored.

N, as she states it. Core: "an analysis of the 55 leaders and most active members of the NWP" (p. 765). By 1950 the majority were white, middle- or upper-class, well educated, professional or semiprofessional, unmarried or widowed, and older (p. 765). General membership about 4000 (1945) to 1400 (1952); "active" 627 (1947) to 200 (1952) (p. 767). A movement appears here only as her sample (National Woman's Party).

## 6. Limits

Single case. She says so. The five internals are "derived from a single case" and treated as hypothetical continua (p. 765). "Although any theory based on a single case is open to challenge," she writes, recent research points toward other 1960s movements (p. 772).

The 1945–mid-1960s NWP "was not successful in its own time" (p. 770). Abeyance is not a success measure. Groups in the holding pattern "may have little impact in their own time" (p. 762). Continuity is the claim, not contemporaneous victory.

She points to civil rights, New Left, and gay rights as places the model might travel (p. 772). That is her pointer, not an extension in this note. Those movements are not opened here and are not scored.
