## 1. Claims, in the authors' terms

Tarrow 2022, Chapter 8 (print pp. 193–210; foundational open). He names a **cycle of contention** as "a phase of heightened conflict across the social system" (p. 195). The rest of that definitional sentence lists what the phase includes: rapid diffusion of collective action from more mobilized to less mobilized sectors; a rapid pace of innovation in forms of contention; new or transformed collective-action frames; a mix of organized and unorganized participation; and intensified information flow and interaction between challengers and authorities (p. 195).

Independent variable (p. 196, "Opportunities, Threats, and Cycles"): political opportunity (plus threat) opening for well-placed "early risers," whose claims resonate with significant others, produce coalitions, and reinforce elite instability, furthered when the state rejects the early risers' claims. He adds that "an opportunity unseen is an opportunity that, for all practical purposes, does not exist" (p. 196).

Dependent claim: discrete conflict generalizes into a system-wide cycle, and the cycle has an outcome that is more than an aggregate of unconnected events. The state must answer with repression, facilitation, or both. "The dynamic of the cycle is the outcome of their interaction" (pp. 195–196).

He refuses a general phase theory: "We do not possess a powerful enough general theory to allow us to delineate the recurring phases in all cycles of contention" (p. 198). Cycles must be disaggregated into practices, campaigns, coalitions, and "the mechanisms of mobilization and demobilization" (p. 198). This edition also names a distinction between "closed" and "transformative" cycles (p. 195).

What happens when a cycle ends, in his terms. Beginnings are often similar; endings are "far more disparate" (p. 196). Most end in restabilization and even disillusionment; others escalate into revolutions or civil wars (p. 196). Participation does not decline at the same pace in every sector: the periphery defects first, the core persists, and "the differential decline of support leads to polarization between those willing to compromise with authorities and those who seek continued confrontation" (pp. 202–203). Citing Jung's re-analysis of Kriesi, he writes that "the demobilization phase of the cycle was marked by a combination of violence and institutionalization" (p. 202). Chapter conclusion: "Most cycles of contention end with some version of restabilization," while some rise to revolt, insurrection, civil war, and revolution (p. 210). He does not claim similar trajectories: "even in very different contexts, similar mechanisms and processes combine to produce different outcomes" (p. 210).

Two endings he contrasts inside the opened chapter (pp. 209–210). Korea: mass protest, especially when it triggers a party-system shift, produced a political realignment. Hong Kong: radicalization of polycentric leadership became the most prominent face; the movement "petered out"; restrictive law followed, culminating in the National Security Law of 30 June 2020; the movement "did not overcome the differences between the more institutionalized and the more radicalized factions" (p. 210). El Salvador and Guatemala are his cases of a transformative cycle, from an ordinary movement cycle into violent confrontation (print ~p. 207).

Fillieule 2015 (critique / review; opening pages opened). Not the foundational claim. He treats demobilization as a plurality: both individual disengagement and collective decline of social movements, ranging up to collapse of a multi-organizational field. He opens by noting how little decline has been studied. He does not collapse failure into "they sold out" and does not collapse it into NRM interiors. That is why the chapter sits here as critique, not as the cycle theory.

Later popular paraphrase (labeled, kept apart). "They sold out," "the leader got greedy," "it died when it went mainstream," "movements just burn out." Tarrow 2022 Ch. 8 does not license those as mechanisms. What he names at the end of a cycle is differential decline, polarization, a pairing of radicalization with institutionalization, repression and facilitation, restabilization, or (in some cases) escalation. Burn-out as a mood is not his term; the closest opened wording is differential decline of support and periphery defection (pp. 202–203).

## 2. Named mechanisms and structures

From Tarrow 2022 Ch. 8 only, in his terms:

- Cycle of contention (p. 195) — a system-wide phase of heightened conflict, not a single movement studied en bloc.
- Early risers / demonstration effect (pp. 197–199) — early claims show authorities' vulnerability, challenge other contenders' interests, and suggest convergences that produce cross-sectoral coalitions; the demonstration effect triggers diffusion, extension, emulation, and reaction among more quiescent groups.
- Diffusion (pp. 198–199) — a propensity for collective action spreads from initiators to unrelated groups and to antagonists; antagonists produce countermovements; extreme cases spiral into retaliatory violence.
- Scale shift (p. 199) — diffusion engages higher levels of the polity, where new opponents, alliances, and institutions can contain contention or make it more transgressive.
- Innovation and its limits (pp. 199–200) — not all innovations survive the end of the cycle; some are peak-tied, some are abandoned when they fail to move elites, some are limited by the threat of repression.
- Radicalization (p. 201) — "a shift in ideological commitments toward the extremes and/or the adoption of more disruptive and more violent forms of contention."
- Institutionalization (pp. 201–202) — "the willingness and ability of leaders from the various actors involved to voluntarily form and maintain a set of rules of engagement, expectations, and tacit understandings based on formal or informal contacts, exchange of information, dialogue, and negotiations." Explicitly paired with radicalization: they "co-occur in many cycles of contention" (p. 202).
- Repression and facilitation (p. 203) — state response takes many forms and varies in effectiveness.
- Differential decline / polarization (pp. 202–203) — uneven withdrawal of support splits compromisers from those who want continued confrontation.
- Demobilization phase (p. 202) — in the Jung/Kriesi re-analysis he cites, marked by a combination of violence and institutionalization.
- Restabilization (pp. 209–210) — the typical ending; not the only ending.
- Closed vs transformative cycles (p. 195; cases ~p. 207, pp. 209–210) — this edition's distinction; transformative cases escalate into violent confrontation.

From Fillieule 2015 only, as critique: demobilization as a plurality of phenomena (individual disengagement through collective decline / field collapse). Not renamed into Tarrow's cycle mechanisms.

Not listed here: exhaustion/factionalization as a 2011-attributed trio; Taylor's abeyance structure; Anderson's five forces; Piven–Cloward disruption; Gamson outcomes. Those works or chapters were not opened for this revision.

## 3. Evidence type and sample

## 6. Limits and overclaims

The opened 2022 chapter licenses a cyclical-contention account of how conflict generalizes and how it can end. It does not license a single phase sequence for every cycle. It does not license "they sold out" or "the leader got greedy" as mechanisms. Institutionalization in his wording is a set of rules of engagement among actors (pp. 201–202), paired with radicalization, not a moral verdict.

It does not license treating movement death as NRM-interior collapse, conversion failure, a commitment-mechanism rundown, or brand-community fade. Those frames are out of scope and absent from the opened chapter.

It does not license a how-to for killing a room or keeping one alive. The chapter is explanatory. Korea vs Hong Kong is a contrast of outcomes, not a playbook.

Restabilization is the typical ending, not a finding that a given room "died." Escalation into revolt or civil war is another ending he names, not a score.

Do not cite this note as an open of Taylor, Anderson, or Tarrow 2011 Part III.

Do not cite Tarrow 2022 Ch. 8 as if Chapters 9–11 were read.

Fillieule 2015 opening pages license a critique of the field's neglect of decline and a plurality framing of demobilization. They do not license a competing cycle theory, and they do not license the Koopmans "weakest chains" line (that line is not in the opened Fillieule pages used here).

## 7. Open questions a later packet-supplement could test

Questions only. No scores. No new primitive names. Do not name the 23 as cases.

When radicalization and institutionalization co-occur (p. 202), what measurable condition makes restabilization more likely than the Hong Kong-style factional split?

Is differential decline of the periphery (pp. 202–203) separable from state repression/facilitation, or does one always carry the other?

Tarrow says endings are more disparate than beginnings (p. 196). What would count as two cycles with the same early-riser pattern and different endings after holding opportunity constant?
